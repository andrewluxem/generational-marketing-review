# Generation Lenses

These are hypothesis sets derived from recurring themes in the source material. They are not universal truths. Apply them only after considering category, geography, life stage, customer state, and first-party evidence.

Birth-year ranges vary across sources. Use the user's definition when provided.

## Gen Z

Questions to test:

- Is the creative native to the selected social or video environment?
- Does the content earn attention through usefulness, entertainment, identity, or participation?
- Are creators credible users rather than one-time endorsers?
- Is product discovery connected to clear proof and an easy mobile path to action?
- Are video, community, gaming, trends, or social participation relevant to the category?
- Does the brand avoid condescension, forced slang, and “always online” stereotypes?

Common risks:

- equating the audience with TikTok;
- using a trend without brand relevance;
- overproduced or deceptive creator content;
- weak product proof;
- inaccessible video;
- assuming low attention rather than improving clarity.

## Zillennials

Questions to test:

- Does the plan reflect a hybrid experience of older social platforms and newer mobile-first behavior?
- Can the campaign use cultural memory or nostalgia without becoming exclusionary?
- Does it balance entertainment with adult-level product clarity?
- Are platform, creator, and shopping assumptions validated rather than borrowed from Gen Z or Millennials?

Common risks:

- treating the cohort as a fixed demographic;
- forcing micro-generation references;
- using nostalgia as the value proposition rather than a creative device.

## Millennials

Questions to test:

- Does the brand demonstrate transparency, responsiveness, and useful community participation?
- Are sustainability, inclusion, and purpose claims specific and substantiated?
- Are creators or artists part of a credible, ongoing relationship?
- Does the content respect the audience rather than chasing trends performatively?
- Is the experience visual, convenient, and informative?

Common risks:

- purpose washing;
- performative social stances;
- assuming every customer prioritizes the same values;
- using “authenticity” as a style without operational proof.

## Xennials

Questions to test:

- Does the plan support cross-device research and decision making?
- Are reviews, product demonstrations, comparisons, specifications, returns, and refunds clear?
- Is checkout simple and trustworthy?
- Can nostalgia improve recall without replacing product relevance?
- Are live content, webinars, or longer demonstrations useful in this category?
- Are creators credible long-term users?

Common risks:

- unsupported nostalgia;
- overcomplicated payment or checkout;
- burying essential product details;
- using age-coded creative instead of customer insight.

## Gen X

Questions to test:

- Does the plan provide practical value, clear proof, and a trustworthy purchase experience?
- Are reviews, comparisons, product demonstrations, delivery, returns, and support visible?
- Are promotions or loyalty benefits clear without assuming price is the only motivator?
- Are established channels such as email, search, YouTube, and major social platforms used for a defined role?
- Does the plan support both concise scanning and deeper research?
- Does the creative avoid “forgotten generation,” technology-resistant, or coupon-only stereotypes?

Common risks:

- assuming the cohort only uses Facebook;
- overusing discounts;
- using old-age or nostalgia clichés;
- weak mobile experience;
- long copy without hierarchy;
- treating fast delivery as a universal requirement.

## Cross-generation rule

When evidence is weak, convert the lens into a testable hypothesis rather than a recommendation.
